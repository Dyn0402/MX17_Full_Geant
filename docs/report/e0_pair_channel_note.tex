\section*{Part I --- Summary of findings}

\begin{center}
\fcolorbox{blue!40!black}{blue!4}{%
\begin{minipage}{0.95\textwidth}
\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{There are two distinct ways a captured neutron makes an $e^+e^-$ pair in
\He{4}, and they are easy to confuse.} They are \emph{not} two decays of one
state --- they are \textbf{different capture channels}, picked out by how the
neutron and \He{3} spins line up:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
  \item \textbf{M1 channel} ($^3S_1\!\to0^+$): emits a real $\gamma$; only
    $\sim$0.35\,\% of those $\gamma$'s convert to a pair. This is the ordinary
    radiative capture (the 54\,$\mu$b). It \emph{grows} toward MeV, so its pairs
    pile up in the \textbf{MeV} region.
  \item \textbf{E0 channel} ($^1S_0\!\to0^+$): a $0^+\!\to0^+$ transition, for
    which a $\gamma$ is \emph{forbidden} --- so it can \emph{only} make a pair,
    \textbf{100\,\%} of the time. It is s-wave and boosted by the 20.21\,MeV
    $0^+$ state just below threshold, so its pairs pile up at \textbf{thermal /
    sub-keV}.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Key consequences:} (i) a $0^+\!\to0^+$ transition can go \emph{only} by
E0 --- not M1 (the same $0\to0$ rule forbids both the photon and M1).
(ii) The E0 channel is \textbf{missing from the standard tools} (ENDF, Geant4)
\emph{and} from our generator --- it is $\gamma$-dark, so nothing counts it.
(iii) Its energy dependence is the \emph{opposite} of the M1/E1 pairs.
(iv) \textbf{Its absolute size is not yet known.} A literature search found the
\He{4} $0^+$ E0 strength is real and measured, but the capture rate has never
been computed; a transparent estimate brackets it at
$\boxed{f=\sE/\sM \sim 10^{-3}\text{--}10^{-2}}$, i.e.\ \textbf{E0 pairs
$\sim$0.3--3$\times$ the ordinary M1 pairs at low neutron energy} --- exactly
Alberto's ``same order of magnitude'' intuition. Pinning the number needs a
\He{4} R-matrix calculation.
\vspace{3pt}
\end{minipage}}
\end{center}

\noindent\textbf{What this note is.} Part~II explains the nuclear physics from
the ground up, in plain terms, with diagrams. Part~III is the magnitude: what we
can and cannot say, the literature dig, and the bracket. The headline picture is
Fig.~\ref{fig:yield}: \textbf{E0 pairs at low $E_n$, M1/E1 pairs at MeV.}

%=============================================================================
\section{What happens when a neutron is captured by \He{3}}

A slow neutron and a \He{3} fuse into an excited \He{4} nucleus
(\He{4}$^*$, $\approx$20.6\,MeV). That excited nucleus almost always just breaks
back apart into $p+{}^3$H (the (n,p)t reaction, $\sim$100\,\% --- and no
pair). Very rarely ($10^{-8}$--$10^{-4}$ of the time) it instead drops to the
\He{4} ground state by emitting electromagnetic radiation --- \textbf{and that
rare drop is the only source of $e^+e^-$ pairs}. Figure~\ref{fig:breakdown}
is the whole logic on one page.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{fig_e0_breakdown}
\caption{What makes the pairs. The neutron+\He{3} fuse; the result almost always
  breaks up to $p+t$. The rare electromagnetic drop to the ground state comes in
  \emph{two flavours}, set by how the two spins line up --- and those two
  flavours are the E0 and M1 channels.}
\label{fig:breakdown}
\end{figure}

%=============================================================================
\section{The selection rule, in plain words}

The \He{4} ground state has spin/parity $0^+$. The transition that makes a pair
goes from \He{4}$^*$ down to that $0^+$ ground state. \textbf{What kind of
radiation is allowed is fixed entirely by the spin and parity of \He{4}$^*$} ---
it is not a free choice.

The crucial fact: \emph{a photon, and an M1, each carry at least one unit of
angular momentum.} A transition between two spin-0 states ($0\to0$) has
\textbf{zero} angular momentum to give away. Therefore, for a $0^+\!\to0^+$
transition:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
  \item \textbf{$\gamma$-ray emission: forbidden} (there is no ``monopole
    photon'').
  \item \textbf{M1: forbidden} --- same reason, it needs $\ge1$ unit.
  \item \textbf{E0: allowed.} E0 is the loophole: the nucleus hands its energy
    straight to an $e^+e^-$ pair through a \emph{virtual} photon, which is
    allowed to be monopole. For a $0^+\!\to0^+$ transition the pair is the
    \emph{only} outlet.
\end{itemize}

\noindent So ``the $0^+$ goes by E0'' is forced --- it cannot go by M1. This is
the point that is easy to trip over: \textbf{the M1 in our problem is a
different transition entirely}, not an alternative way for the $0^+$ to decay.

%=============================================================================
\section{The two channels: E0 vs M1}

When the neutron (spin $\tfrac12$) and \He{3} (spin $\tfrac12$) capture in
s-wave, their spins can be \emph{opposite} or \emph{aligned}, and that decides
which \He{4}$^*$ state you build:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\hline
spins & channel & \He{4}$^*$ is & $\to$ ground state \\
\hline
\textbf{opposite} & $^1S_0$ & $0^+$ & \textbf{E0}: $\gamma$ forbidden $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{100\,\% pair} ($\gamma$-dark) \\
\textbf{aligned}  & $^3S_1$ & $1^+$ & \textbf{M1}: $\gamma$ allowed $\Rightarrow$ $\gamma$ (99.65\,\%) $+$ 0.35\,\% pair \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent These are \textbf{two parallel doorways}. The all-important practical
consequence: the famous radiative-capture number (54\,$\mu$b at thermal, ENDF
MT=102) is \emph{only the M1 ($1^+$) channel} --- it counts $\gamma$-emitting
captures. The E0 ($0^+$) channel emits no $\gamma$, so \textbf{it is invisible
to that number, to ENDF, and to Geant4}, and it is not in our event generator
either. It is missing in three places at once.

The M1 channel's pairs are the 0.35\,\% internal-pair-conversion (IPC) tail of
its $\gamma$: at $\sim$20\,MeV the IPC coefficient is
$\alpha_{\rm IPC}\approx3.5\times10^{-3}$ (anchored to the \emph{measured}
$^{12}$C 15.1\,MeV M1 value $(3.3\pm0.5)\times10^{-3}$ and the $^{8}$Be
18.15\,MeV line; BrIcc's tables stop at 6\,MeV so they cannot be used at our
energy --- see Part~III).

%=============================================================================
\section{Why angular momentum sets the energy dependence}

Now the energy dependence, which is where it gets interesting. To build a given
state you need a specific orbital angular momentum $\ell$, because the parity of
the $n+{}$\He{3} system is $(-1)^\ell$:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
  \item $0^+$ (parity $+$) needs $\ell$ even $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{s-wave}
    ($\ell=0$).
  \item $0^-$ (parity $-$) needs $\ell$ odd $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{p-wave}
    ($\ell=1$).
\end{itemize}
An s-wave neutron has \emph{no centrifugal barrier} --- it gets in for free, and
the formation rate behaves as $\sim1/v$, \textbf{growing without limit toward
low energy}. A p-wave neutron must climb the barrier, so its formation
\emph{vanishes} at threshold and only switches on with energy. Figure~\ref{fig:doorway}
makes this concrete with a Breit--Wigner $\times$ penetrability calculation using
the measured \He{4} resonance parameters.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{fig_e0_doorway}
\caption{Relative strength for forming the $0^+$ (s-wave) vs $0^-$ (p-wave)
  doorway, and the underlying penetrabilities (bottom). The $0^+$ dominates
  everywhere below $\sim$0.5\,MeV --- by $\sim$2500$\times$ at 1\,keV. The $0^-$
  is \emph{not} forbidden below 1\,MeV; it is barrier-suppressed and in fact
  \emph{peaks} at $E_n\approx0.64$\,MeV. (Single-level illustration; absolute
  vertical offsets need the full R-matrix.)}
\label{fig:doorway}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The $0^-$ question, settled.} A natural worry is whether the $0^-$
state (21.0\,MeV) is ``energetically forbidden'' at low energy. It is not: the
$n+{}$\He{3} channel is open at all $E_n>0$. What suppresses the $0^-$ below
$\sim$0.5\,MeV is the \emph{p-wave barrier}, not energetics --- and it actually
peaks just below 1\,MeV. (As a bonus, the $0^-$ is a \emph{spectator} for the
pair signal anyway: $0^-\!\to0^+$ is forbidden for $\gamma$, M1 \emph{and} E0,
so it simply decays back by particle emission.) \textbf{The upshot: as the
neutron energy drops, we make relatively more of the $0^+$ (E0) doorway.}

%=============================================================================
\section{The cross-section backdrop}

For context, Fig.~\ref{fig:xs} shows the two known \He{3}$+n$ cross sections.
The (n,p)t dominates utterly ($1/v$, 5333\,b at thermal). The radiative
$\sng$ (the M1/E1, 54\,$\mu$b at thermal) is $1/v$ at low energy then
\emph{rises} toward the MeV region as the E1 / giant-dipole strength turns on ---
the ratio $\sng/\snp$ climbs $\sim$10$^4\times$ from thermal to 10\,MeV. That
rise is \emph{why the ordinary M1/E1 pairs are a MeV phenomenon}. Note this is
the \emph{lumped} radiative number; it contains no E0 (which is $\gamma$-dark)
and cannot be sliced into multipoles.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.74\textwidth]{fig_e0_he3xs}
\caption{\He{3}$+n$ cross sections (ENDF/B-VIII.0). Bottom: the radiative/(n,p)
  ratio is flat at $10^{-8}$ through the sub-keV window and climbs to
  $\sim2\times10^{-4}$ by 10\,MeV --- the E1 turning on. The E0 channel is not
  in this file.}
\label{fig:xs}
\end{figure}

%=============================================================================
\section{Where the pairs come from: the punchline}

Putting it together: the two pair channels live at \textbf{opposite ends} of the
energy range (Fig.~\ref{fig:yield}).
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
  \item \textbf{M1/E1 (blue):} rides the rising $\sng/\snp$, so it
    \textbf{peaks in the MeV region}. This is the piece we can already compute
    from the simulated capture rate $\times\,\alpha_{\rm IPC}$
    ($\approx$2200 pairs/day, concentrated 0.1--10\,MeV).
  \item \textbf{E0 (red):} pure s-wave, boosted by the sub-threshold $0^+$, and
    with \emph{no} 0.35\,\% penalty (the $\gamma$ is forbidden, so every such
    capture is a pair) --- so it \textbf{piles up at thermal / sub-keV} and
    fades upward.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{fig_e0_yield}
\caption{$e^+e^-$ pairs/day vs neutron energy. M1/E1 (blue) peaks at MeV; E0
  (red, three assumed strengths) piles up at low $E_n$. \textbf{Below
  $\sim$1\,keV the E0 channel can dominate the pair yield} even at a few-percent
  strength, because it dodges the 0.35\,\% IPC suppression. The vertical
  position of the red curves is the one unknown (Part~III).}
\label{fig:yield}
\end{figure}

\noindent The striking implication: \textbf{the sub-keV region we had written
off} (for M1 it is dead --- self-shielded, capped at $10^{-8}$) \textbf{might be
exactly where the E0 pairs are.} That is a genuinely different place to look.

%=============================================================================
\section{Part III --- How big is the E0 channel? (the open question)}

Everything above --- the channel structure, the selection rules, the opposite
energy trends --- is \textbf{settled}. The one thing still unknown is the
\emph{height} of the red curve: how strong the E0 ($^1S_0$) capture is relative
to the M1 ($^3S_1$), i.e.\ $f=\sE/\sM$.

\subsection*{What the literature dig found}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
  \item The \He{4} $0^+_2$ (20.21\,MeV) \textbf{E0 strength is real and
    measured}: its monopole transition form factor was measured precisely at
    MAMI \cite{kegel}, and is the subject of the ``$\alpha$-particle monopole
    puzzle'' \cite{puzzle}. So the \emph{decay-side} E0 matrix element is
    established --- it is a genuine \emph{collective} monopole (``breathing'')
    transition, \textbf{not weak}.
  \item But that form factor is the \emph{bound} $0^+_2\!\to$g.s.\ strength.
    Neutron capture needs the $^1S_0$ \emph{continuum}$\to$g.s.\ amplitude at
    20.58\,MeV (the $0^+_2$ sits 0.37\,MeV \emph{below} threshold and enters
    through its sub-threshold tail). Converting one to the other is reaction
    theory, not a table lookup.
  \item The ATOMKI \He{4} pair experiment ($^3$H$(p,e^+e^-)$\He{4}
    \cite{atomki}) models its pairs as the internal/external pair creation of
    \emph{direct E1/M1 proton capture} --- it does not isolate a clean E0 line,
    and the $p+t$ entrance couples to the $0^+_2$ differently than $n+{}$\He{3}.
  \item I found \textbf{no calculation of \He{3}$(n,e^+e^-)$\He{4}} (the E0
    capture) anywhere --- this specific channel appears genuinely un-computed.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{A transparent bracket}
Since no number exists, here is an honest order-of-magnitude argument. The
decisive quantity is the ratio of \emph{pair} yields, $f/\alpha_{\rm IPC}$:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
  \item \textbf{Lower edge:} E0 is allowed and collective, and unpolarised
    $n+{}$\He{3} populates the $^1S_0$ channel with statistical weight
    $\tfrac14$ (vs $\tfrac34$ for $^3S_1$). So $f$ is unlikely to be far below
    the $\alpha_{\rm IPC}\sim10^{-3}$ scale.
  \item \textbf{Upper edge:} E0 pair emission has \emph{no real-photon mode}, so
    it is intrinsically much slower than the M1 \emph{photon} width --- an E0
    pair width is typically $\sim10^{-2}$--$10^{-3}$ of a comparable allowed M1
    photon width. Even with the collective enhancement, $f\gtrsim0.1$ is hard to
    reach.
\end{itemize}
\[
\boxed{\,f=\sE/\sM \sim 10^{-3}\text{--}10^{-2}\,}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\frac{\text{E0 pairs}}{\text{M1 pairs}}=\frac{f}{\alpha_{\rm IPC}}
\sim 0.3\text{--}3 \ \ (\text{up to}\sim30)
\]
at the low neutron energies where E0 lives. \textbf{So E0 is plausibly a
same-order-or-larger contributor to the sub-keV pair yield} --- which is exactly
the ``same order of magnitude'' point that started this. The three red curves in
Fig.~\ref{fig:yield} ($f=0.003/0.03/0.3$) bracket this range.

\subsection*{What would replace the bracket with a number}
One calculation: a \textbf{\He{4} multi-level R-matrix} (G.~M.~Hale maintains the
evaluated \He{4} system) for the $^1S_0$ $n+{}$\He{3}$\to$g.s.\ E0 capture,
\emph{normalised} by the measured (e,e$'$) monopole strength and the $0^+_2$
neutron asymptotic normalisation. That is a concrete, well-posed ask for a
\He{4}-theory contact, and is the clean next step if we want to firm up the red
curve. \emph{(Worth keeping on the list to work on together.)}
